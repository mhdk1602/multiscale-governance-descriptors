\begin{abstract}
Data governance maturity is typically assessed through surveys, audits,
and capability-model checklists. These methods capture stated policy but
not realized practice. We propose four multi-scale structural descriptors
that measure governance quality directly from the topology of data lineage
graphs: community stability under resolution sweep (D1), blast-radius
concentration profile (D2), spectral gap and Fiedler structure (D3), and
persistent homology of shortest-path filtrations (D4). In controlled
experiments on synthetic lineage graphs at three governance quality levels,
all four descriptors achieve statistically significant differentiation
($p < 0.001$, Mann--Whitney $U$), with Cohen's $d$ ranging from 1.7 to
16.0 at the largest scale tested ($N = 200$). The descriptors respond to
governance-relevant interventions (domain boundary reorganization,
orphan removal) with signal-to-noise ratios exceeding 6.5, while remaining
insensitive to random perturbations of comparable magnitude. Multi-scale
features explain 75\% more variance in Mean-Time-to-Detect simulations
than single-scale baselines ($R^2 = 0.307$ vs.\ $0.175$). Validation on
an anonymized production dbt lineage graph (223 nodes, 263 edges, 26
domains) yields Spearman correlations between D3 descriptors and
governance metadata (documentation rate $\rho = -0.71$, $p = 0.001$;
composite governance score $\rho = -0.64$, $p = 0.004$). These results
suggest that lineage topology encodes recoverable governance signal,
opening a path toward continuous, audit-free governance monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data governance frameworks specify how organizations should manage data
assets: who owns them, how quality is assured, what documentation exists,
and how changes propagate through transformation pipelines. Mature
governance manifests as clear domain boundaries, tested transformations,
and documented interfaces between stages of a data pipeline. Immature
governance manifests as orphaned assets, unclear ownership, redundant
paths, and untested transformations.

The dominant approach to measuring governance maturity relies on
self-assessment against capability models such as DAMA-DMBOK or DCAM.
These instruments capture stated policy. They do not capture the actual
structural organization of data assets. Two organizations with identical
DMBOK maturity scores can have radically different lineage topologies:
one with clean layered DAGs and well-separated domain boundaries, another
with tangled cross-domain dependencies and redundant paths.

We hypothesize that governance maturity leaves structural fingerprints in
data lineage graphs. If domain boundaries are well-enforced, community
detection at the appropriate resolution should recover stable, modular
partitions. If transformations are well-tested, the blast radius of any
single failure should be concentrated rather than dispersed. If the
pipeline architecture is clean, the algebraic connectivity of the lineage
graph should reflect appropriate coupling between components. If
redundant paths exist (a sign of technical debt or unclear ownership),
persistent homology should detect cycles in the undirected skeleton.

This paper introduces four multi-scale structural descriptors designed
to capture these properties, validates them on synthetic lineage graphs
with controlled governance variation, tests their sensitivity to
governance-relevant interventions, and provides initial validation on
a real production dbt lineage graph.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Four graph-theoretic descriptors (D1--D4) that measure governance
        quality from lineage topology, each operating at a different
        structural scale. To our knowledge, no prior work applies
        community stability, blast-radius Gini profiles, spectral gap
        analysis, or persistent homology to data governance measurement.

  \item An empirical validation framework with synthetic lineage generators
        at three governance quality levels, demonstrating statistically
        significant differentiation and intervention sensitivity.

  \item A predictive comparison showing that multi-scale structural
        features explain substantially more variance in simulated
        governance outcomes (Mean-Time-to-Detect) than single-scale
        graph statistics.

  \item Initial real-data validation on an anonymized production dbt
        lineage graph with genuine governance metadata.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Governance Measurement}

Data governance frameworks define structural and procedural mechanisms
for data management \citep{khatri2010,abraham2019}. Assessment relies on
maturity models: DAMA-DMBOK prescribes five levels across eleven
knowledge areas; DCAM evaluates institutional readiness for data
management. \citet{weber2009} argue that governance design is contingent
on organizational strategy, implying that a single maturity scale is
insufficient.

Institutional theory provides a theoretical lens. \citet{dimaggio1983}
describe three isomorphic pressures (coercive, mimetic, normative) that
cause organizations to converge structurally under shared governance
regimes. \citet{scott2013} extends this via regulative, normative, and
cultural-cognitive pillars. Recent work positions governance principles
as emergent institutional arrangements rather than top-down mandates.
We operationalize this view: if governance is an institutional
arrangement, it should produce measurable structural signatures in the
artifacts it governs.

\subsection{Graph Analysis of Data Pipelines}

\citet{li2025} apply temporal convolutional and graph convolutional
networks to data lineage DAGs for fault classification (1,240 nodes,
5,870 edges), achieving 95.8\% accuracy. Their work uses lineage
structure as input to prediction but does not measure governance
quality from that structure.

Knowledge graph quality metrics \citep{seo2022} define six structural
measures for knowledge graphs. \citet{tiddi2024} position knowledge
graphs as governance backbones for AI systems. Neither applies
multi-scale graph descriptors to lineage topology.

\subsection{Community Detection at Multiple Resolutions}

The resolution parameter in modularity optimization
\citep{reichardt2006} controls community granularity.
\citet{lambiotte2014} interpret the parameter as Markov diffusion time.
\citet{delvenne2010} introduce stability as plateau detection via
normalized variation of information (NVI). The Leiden algorithm
\citep{traag2019} guarantees connected communities, addressing a known
Louvain failure mode. We adapt the resolution sweep as a governance
descriptor by measuring the stability of recovered communities across
the full resolution range.

\subsection{Spectral Methods}

Algebraic connectivity (the second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue)
measures graph cohesion \citep{fiedler1973}. Spectral gap correlates
with robustness to random failure and inversely with mean path length.
Von Neumann spectral entropy \citep{domenico2016} quantifies complexity
of the eigenvalue distribution. We compose these into a governance
descriptor that captures coupling structure between pipeline components.

\subsection{Persistent Homology on Networks}

Topological data analysis via persistent homology has been applied to
brain networks, social networks, and protein interaction networks
\citep{otter2017,petri2013}. \citet{chowdhury2018} introduce directed
persistent path homology. For DAGs, directed H1 is trivially zero (no
directed cycles by definition), but H1 in the undirected skeleton
detects redundant dependency paths. To our knowledge, no prior work
applies persistent homology to data lineage or governance graphs.

\section{Descriptors}

We define four structural descriptors for directed acyclic data
lineage graphs $G = (V, E)$ with $|V| = N$ nodes and $|E| = M$ edges.
Each node represents a data asset (source, transformation, or mart).
Edges represent dependency: $(u, v) \in E$ means $v$ depends on $u$.

\subsection{D1: Community Stability Index (CSI)}

We sweep the resolution parameter $\gamma$ of the Leiden algorithm over
$[0.1, 3.0]$ on the undirected skeleton of $G$, computing community
partitions $\mathcal{C}(\gamma)$ at each resolution. For adjacent
resolution pairs $(\gamma_i, \gamma_{i+1})$, we compute the normalized
variation of information:

\begin{equation}
  \text{NVI}(\gamma_i, \gamma_{i+1})
  = \frac{H(\mathcal{C}_i | \mathcal{C}_{i+1})
          + H(\mathcal{C}_{i+1} | \mathcal{C}_i)}
         {H(\mathcal{C}_i, \mathcal{C}_{i+1})}
\end{equation}

The Community Stability Index aggregates this into a single scalar:

\begin{equation}
  \text{CSI} = 1 - \text{mean}(\text{NVI}(\gamma_i, \gamma_{i+1}))
\end{equation}

CSI $\in [0, 1]$, with higher values indicating more stable community
structure. We additionally extract fragmentation onset (the $\gamma$
at which the number of communities first exceeds 1), modularity at
$\gamma = 1$, and number of communities at $\gamma = 1$.

\textbf{Governance interpretation.} Well-governed pipelines with clear
domain boundaries should exhibit high CSI (communities are stable across
resolutions, reflecting genuine organizational structure). Poorly governed
pipelines with tangled cross-domain dependencies should exhibit low CSI
(communities shift erratically as resolution changes).

\subsection{D2: Blast-Radius Concentration Profile}

For each node $v \in V$ and depth $d \in \{1, 2, \ldots\}$, we compute
$R_d(v) = |\{u : \text{dist}(v, u) = d\}|$, the number of nodes
reachable at exactly depth $d$ downstream. The Gini coefficient of
$\{R_d(v)\}_{v \in V}$ at each depth $d$ yields a concentration profile:

\begin{equation}
  G(d) = \text{Gini}(\{R_d(v) : v \in V\})
\end{equation}

We extract the maximum Gini across depths and the concentration
transition depth (the depth at which Gini first exceeds 0.5, or $-1$ if
never reached).

\textbf{Governance interpretation.} In well-governed pipelines, blast
radius should be concentrated: a few hub nodes affect many downstream
assets, while most nodes have limited impact. High max Gini indicates
concentrated risk, which, when combined with stewardship data, reflects
intentional architectural design. Low max Gini suggests diffuse,
unpredictable failure propagation.

\subsection{D3: Spectral Gap and Fiedler Structure}

From the normalized Laplacian $\mathcal{L}$ of the undirected skeleton,
we extract:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Algebraic connectivity $\lambda_2$: the smallest nonzero
        eigenvalue.
  \item Normalized spectral gap: $\lambda_2 / N$.
  \item Fiedler bimodality: bimodality coefficient of the Fiedler vector
        (the eigenvector associated with $\lambda_2$).
  \item Spectral entropy: von Neumann entropy of the normalized
        eigenvalue distribution, $S = -\sum_i \bar{\lambda}_i
        \log \bar{\lambda}_i$ where
        $\bar{\lambda}_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Governance interpretation.} Algebraic connectivity measures
overall graph cohesion. Low values indicate loosely connected components
(possibly orphaned domains). High Fiedler bimodality indicates a natural
bipartition, potentially reflecting a two-tier architecture (e.g.,
source vs.\ mart layers). Spectral entropy captures the complexity of
the coupling structure.

\subsection{D4: Persistent Homology via Shortest-Path Filtration}

We compute the all-pairs shortest-path distance matrix on the
undirected skeleton of $G$ and construct a Rips complex filtration. For
each filtration threshold $\epsilon$, we compute homology groups in
dimensions 0 and 1. The resulting persistence diagram records birth and
death times for each topological feature.

From the H1 (cycle) persistence diagram, we extract:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Number of H1 bars ($n_{\text{H1}}$) and its normalization
        $n_{\text{H1}} / N$.
  \item Total H1 persistence:
        $P_1 = \sum_i (d_i - b_i)$ over all H1 bars.
  \item H1 persistence entropy:
        $S_1 = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ where
        $p_i = (d_i - b_i) / P_1$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Governance interpretation.} H1 features in the undirected
skeleton represent redundant dependency paths: alternative routes between
nodes that do not exist in a clean tree-structured pipeline. High
$n_{\text{H1}} / N$ indicates structural redundancy, which may reflect
technical debt, unclear ownership, or organic growth without
architectural oversight.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Synthetic Lineage Generator}

We generate directed acyclic lineage graphs at four scales
($N \in \{30, 50, 100, 200\}$) and three governance quality levels:

\begin{description}
  \item[Well-governed:] Three clearly separated domains with layered
    structure (source $\to$ staging $\to$ mart). Cross-domain edges
    only at mart layer. Test and documentation coverage assigned to
    70--90\% of nodes.

  \item[Baseline:] Same domain count but with moderate cross-domain
    coupling at all layers. Test coverage 40--60\%.

  \item[Poorly governed:] Flat structure with frequent cross-domain
    shortcuts and orphaned nodes. Test coverage 10--30\%. Random edge
    additions creating shortcut paths.
\end{description}

Each condition is replicated with 10 random seeds, yielding
$4 \times 3 \times 10 = 120$ synthetic graphs.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Governance Differentiation}

For each graph, we compute all four descriptors (D1--D4), nine
single-scale baselines (mean/max/std of degree, betweenness, and
PageRank; diameter; density), and three MTTD-based outcome measures
(monitor placement by stewardship, betweenness, and blast-radius
strategies). Mann--Whitney $U$ tests compare well-governed vs.\
poorly governed at each scale, with Cohen's $d$ for effect size.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Real Data Validation}

We validate on two real lineage topologies:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An anonymized production dbt manifest lineage (223 nodes, 263
        edges, 26 domains) exported from a production dbt project using
        HMAC-SHA256 anonymization. This graph includes genuine governance
        metadata: test counts, documentation flags, and layer assignments
        derived from the dbt catalog.

  \item A static-code-derived ML pipeline topology (86 nodes, 97 edges,
        14 domains) with observation-based governance placeholders.
\end{enumerate}

For the dbt lineage, we partition by domain and compute Spearman
correlations between per-domain descriptor values and governance
metadata (test rate, documentation rate, composite governance score).

\subsection{Experiment 3: Intervention Sensitivity}

We apply four governance-relevant interventions to a baseline synthetic
graph ($N = 132$, 3 domains):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Add domains:} Reorganize a fraction of nodes into new
        domain boundaries.
  \item \textbf{Remove orphans:} Delete nodes with no incoming or
        outgoing edges.
  \item \textbf{Remove shortcuts:} Delete edges that skip architectural
        layers.
  \item \textbf{Add monitors:} Place quality monitors at high-centrality
        nodes.
\end{enumerate}

Each intervention is compared against 60 random perturbation trials
(edge removal, addition, and rewiring at the same magnitude). The
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is the intervention effect size divided by
the standard deviation of random perturbation effects.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Predictive Comparison}

We compare multi-scale features (D1--D4) against single-scale baselines
in two prediction tasks:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classification:} Logistic regression and random forest
        predicting governance quality level (well vs.\ poor).
  \item \textbf{Regression:} Ridge and random forest predicting
        Mean-Time-to-Detect from structural features.
\end{itemize}

All models are evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Phase 1: Descriptor Validation}

All four descriptors produce interpretable output on known graph
families (complete graphs, barbell graphs, star DAGs, path graphs,
Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi random graphs, and Zachary's karate club) at
$N = 30$--$200$. D1 recovers the known two-community structure of the
karate club (CSI = 0.886). D3 correctly identifies the complete graph
as maximally connected ($\lambda_2 = N$) and the barbell as having near-zero
algebraic connectivity. D4 detects cycles in the grid graph and reports
zero H1 for tree structures.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the Descriptors Measure}

The four descriptors capture governance quality at complementary
structural scales. D1 (community stability) measures whether domain
boundaries are architecturally enforced or merely nominal. D2
(blast-radius concentration) measures whether failure impact is
predictable and concentrated. D3 (spectral structure) measures the
coupling strength between pipeline components. D4 (persistent homology)
measures the prevalence of redundant dependency paths.

The real-data validation reveals that these dimensions are partially
independent. In the production dbt lineage, D3 (spectral) correlates
with documentation rate while D4 (topological) correlates with test
rate. This makes organizational sense: documentation is about making
existing structure legible (captured by connectivity patterns), while
testing is about validating specific dependency paths (captured by
topological features).

\subsection{The Negative D3--Governance Correlation}

The negative correlation between D3 algebraic connectivity and
documentation rate ($\rho = -0.71$) warrants explanation. In this
production system, source-only domains have moderate documentation
(41\%) but zero internal edges, yielding algebraic connectivity of
zero. Transformation-heavy domains (silver/intermediate) have high
internal connectivity but zero documentation. The well-governed
gold/mart domain sits in between. This pattern reflects a genuine
organizational dynamic: documentation effort is concentrated at system
boundaries (data ingestion) and final outputs (marts), not at
intermediate transformations.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Single production instance.} The real-data validation uses one
organization's dbt lineage. Cross-organizational generalization requires
additional datasets. The open datasets identified in our literature
review (DW-Bench, Huawei DLG, WfCommons) could extend validation.

\textbf{Steward metadata absent.} The production dbt catalog contains
no steward or owner metadata, reducing the composite governance score
to a two-component measure (tests + documentation). Organizations with
richer metadata would enable testing D1's hypothesized association with
ownership structure.

\textbf{Domain-level sparsity.} Of 26 domains, only 6 have internal
edges sufficient for meaningful D1 computation. The remaining 20 are
source-only clusters with degenerate descriptor values. Larger or more
architecturally complex lineage graphs would provide more per-domain
variation.

\textbf{Synthetic generator validity.} The governance variation in our
synthetic generators is architectural (edge patterns, domain boundaries),
not metadata-only. This is by design: the descriptors measure structure,
not metadata. However, the generators produce cleaner separation between
governance levels than likely exists in practice, contributing to the
classification ceiling effect.

\textbf{Causal direction undetermined.} The correlations between
descriptors and governance metadata could reflect either direction:
governance practices producing cleaner structure, or cleaner structure
enabling easier governance. Our design measures association, not
causation.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

If validated on additional datasets, these descriptors could enable:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Continuous governance monitoring.} Computing D1--D4 on
        each CI/CD push provides a structural governance score without
        requiring manual assessment.
  \item \textbf{Governance drift detection.} CSI degradation over time
        signals erosion of domain boundaries. Increasing H1/N signals
        accumulating technical debt through redundant paths.
  \item \textbf{Targeted intervention.} D3 identifies structurally
        isolated components. D2 identifies nodes with outsized blast
        radius. These guide where to invest governance effort.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced four multi-scale structural descriptors for measuring data
governance quality from lineage graph topology. Synthetic experiments
confirm that all four descriptors differentiate governance quality levels
with statistical significance and respond to governance-relevant
interventions above random noise. Real-data validation on a production
dbt lineage graph yields statistically significant correlations between
structural descriptors and governance metadata. Multi-scale features
explain 75\% more outcome variance than single-scale baselines.

These findings suggest that lineage topology encodes recoverable
governance signal. The descriptors are computable from any lineage graph
(dbt manifests, data catalog exports, code-derived dependency graphs)
and require no access to the underlying data. They complement, rather
than replace, existing governance frameworks by providing continuous
structural measurement where traditional methods provide periodic
assessment.
